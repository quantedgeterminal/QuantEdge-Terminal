Research · measured on dataset-v1

Does speed pay on a Solana on-chain order book?

Not at a 1.5-second book grain. On the two Manifest markets whose book changes about every 1.5 seconds, a taker strategy with a zero-delay feed made less than the same strategy 400 ms late. On the third market, whose book changes twice as often, the faster trader lost slightly less — a consistent difference too small to trade on. What follows is the method, why the data cannot see finer than one book state, and the code that reproduces every number on this page.

Data
dataset-v1: 106,440 book states, 15.57 h, cbBTC · RENDER · USDT against USDC
Strategies
the terminal’s three taker presets, plus a control built so that speed is an edge

0 · 50 · 100 · 200 · 400 ms

171 measured: 18 at defaults, 135 in the sweep, 18 of the control
Reproduce
one command, no database or keys, about 15 s

The answer, market by market

Each preset ran at its defaults (100 USDC orders, 10 bp slippage limit) over ten back-to-back 1.5-hour windows. The effect is P&L at 0 ms minus P&L at 400 ms in the same window: negative means the faster trader made less.

Three things are visible before any statistics. Every strategy lost money even with no delay at all, so the delay never decided whether a strategy works, only how much it lost. On cbBTC and RENDER the faster feed made the loss bigger in nearly every window. On USDT the faster feed made it smaller in every window for queue depletion — by 0.003 bp of what was traded.

Why: the book has a grain

A Manifest book is one on-chain account. Its state changes when a transaction touches it, and the recording keeps every state the RPC providers delivered. On all three markets each state sat in its own slot (1.00 states per slot on each), and the median gap between states was 1,523 ms on cbBTC, 1,520 ms on RENDER and 755 ms on USDT.

A delay shorter than that gap can only do one thing: show the previous state instead of the current one. That is what the runs show. At every delay from 50 to 400 ms the strategy saw a different state than at 0 ms on 100% of steps, on every market. Between 50 and 100 ms it saw a different state on 0.00% of steps on cbBTC, and 400 ms differed from 200 ms on only 0.91% of steps on cbBTC and 1.35% on RENDER — but on 31.18% on USDT, whose book moves twice as fast.

So the comparison “0 versus 400 ms” on cbBTC and RENDER is really “the current state versus the one before it”: about 1.5 seconds of market time, not 400 ms. The levels in between cannot be told apart by this data, however finely they are spaced; on all 9 market and strategy pairs, 1, 10 and 100 ms gave identical results (9 of 9).

A cost per 100 ms is a choice of grid

The terminal reports a “cost of 100 ms” as the slope between the fastest and slowest level. Because almost all of the effect happens in the first step, the slope depends on where the grid ends. Over the whole recording, imbalance momentum on cbBTC gives 38.95 USDC per 100 ms on a grid ending at 400 ms and 0.48 on one ending at 60,000 ms; on RENDER, 128.42 against 1.88. Both are correct slopes. Neither is a price of 100 ms, which is why the terminal prints the grid next to the number and names the levels the data cannot tell apart.

What the 0 ms row is

At zero delay the strategy is filled against exactly the book it read. Its quote cannot move away before the order lands, so it never misses: across all 171 configurations, 1,366,194 orders at 0 ms and 0 left unfilled. That is a property of the execution model, not of a trader. No real feed reaches the row; it is the reference the other rows are measured from.

The delayed rows show why being late was not worse here. In the sweep below, the average slippage at 400 ms was negative — the order filled better than the price the strategy saw — in 81 of the 120 configurations that traded at all. That fits a market that partly reverts within the 1.5 seconds between states: a strategy that chases what it just saw does better arriving after part of the move has given back. It is consistent with the data; the data alone does not prove it.

Changing the size and the limit

The defaults could hide an effect, so every preset was rerun on every market with five order sizes (100, 1,000, 10,000, 100,000 and 1,000,000 USDC) and three slippage limits (10, 2 and 1 bp): 135 configurations on the independent 1.5-hour windows.

  • The faster trader came out ahead on average in 18 of them. A sign test across windows found a consistent direction in 49 configurations where speed lost and 9 where it won.
  • All 9 wins are queue depletion on USDT. At 1,000,000 USDC orders the faster trader lost 198.69 USDC less per window, in every window, out of a total loss of −97,752.37: 0.008 bp of turnover.
  • The largest win outside USDT was imbalance momentum on RENDER at 10,000 USDC and a 2 bp limit: 170.02 USDC per window on average, faster ahead in 7 of 10 windows (p = 0.344, mean over its standard error 1.17). With a 1 bp limit the same strategy gives −18.21.
  • Size runs out as a lever. On cbBTC the results stop changing above 10,000 USDC and on RENDER above 100,000: within the slippage limit those books hold less than that, so a bigger order fills the same levels. The USDT book is deep enough that size keeps mattering.

A control where speed should win

If the setup simply could not reward speed, the result above would say nothing. So the study adds a strategy built to profit from it: take a quote that sits on the wrong side of where the book just was — an ask at or below the previous state’s mid, less an edge of 0, 2 or 10 bp, or a bid above it — before anyone else does. A trader with no delay takes it from the book it read; a late one finds it gone. It runs only in the study, not in the terminal.

Even the strategy designed to reward speed did not reliably do so on cbBTC or RENDER. The only market that leans its way is again USDT, again by cents. The stale quotes it hunts are rarely there long enough, at this grain, for zero delay to be the difference.

Three statistics, three questions

  • Is the effect bigger than the noise between windows? The bar set before the first run: the effect must exceed three standard deviations of P&L across windows. It did in 0 of 171 configurations.
  • Can its size be predicted for a new window? Three standard deviations of the per-window effect itself: 0 of 171. The effect varies from window to window about as much as it is large.
  • Is there an effect, and which way? An exact sign test on the independent windows. This is the one that answers: yes, on several strategies, and on cbBTC and RENDER it points against speed.

The overlapping 6-hour windows of the first measurement are reported in the published results too, but the sign test and standard errors are read on the back-to-back 1.5-hour windows, because ten windows that share five hours of six are not ten samples.

What this does not say

  • That speed does not pay on Solana. This is one venue, Manifest, over one night, 11–12 September 2026.
  • Anything about makers. The strategies only take liquidity; without a trade tape, queue position cannot be modelled honestly.
  • Anything finer than a book state. A venue or a recording with more states per second could show what this one cannot — and USDT, the finest-grained market here, is the one where speed leaned positive.
  • That the fills are real. Orders are filled against the recorded top 15 levels and leave the book as it was; a real order would move it.

Reproduce it

The study reads only the published CSV files of release dataset-v1, checks them against SHA256SUMS, and runs the terminal’s own backtest engine on them. Node 22 or later and pnpm:

git clone https://github.com/quantedgeterminal/QuantEdge-Terminal
cd QuantEdge-Terminal && pnpm install
gh release download dataset-v1 -R quantedgeterminal/QuantEdge-Terminal \
  -p '*.csv.gz' -p SHA256SUMS -D data/dataset-v1
pnpm --filter @quantedge/study study --data data/dataset-v1 --out study.json

The output is byte-for-byte the results file in the repository, and every number on this page is read from it; a test fails the build if the two disagree. The code is apps/study, under the Apache License 2.0.

Corrections to figures published earlier

The first write-up of this measurement, on the main page and in the README, was produced by scripts that were not kept. Rewriting them against the open dataset reproduced the counts, the grain, the zero-delay row and the sweep’s largest candidate exactly, and changed four statements:

  • “180 configurations”: the sweep as recorded had 90 (three presets, five sizes, three limits, two markets). This study runs 135, adding USDT.
  • “Average slippage at 400 ms is −1 to −9 bp”: negative in 81 of 120 configurations that traded, ranging from −569.26 to 3.94 bp.
  • “P&L at 0 ms is lower in 10 of 10 windows” and “did not make more money with a faster feed”: true of cbBTC and RENDER, not of queue depletion on USDT, where the faster trader lost less in every window.
  • “400 ms differs from 200 ms on 0.87% of steps”: 0.91% on cbBTC, 1.35% on RENDER, 31.18% on USDT.

The conclusion did not change. Its scope is now stated per market.
